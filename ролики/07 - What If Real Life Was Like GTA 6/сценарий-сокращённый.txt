Monday, eight ten in the morning, room one-fourteen. Leon walks in late, and something is wrong with the floor. A yellow circle glows beside his desk, and above it, floating in the air, white letters. Mission. Pass the math test. Reward, twenty thousand dollars. Leon looks around. Nobody else sees it. Ms. Park hands out the papers. Leon writes his name, and for the first time in his life, he knows every answer. Forty-five minutes later, a green number pops up over his head. Plus twenty thousand dollars. His phone buzzes. Bank alert. That's almost four months of a normal paycheck, for one test. Chase leans over. "Did you just win the lottery?" Leon is ten years old, and he has no idea what is happening. But by Friday, he'll own a garage, a helicopter, and the whole school's respect.

So, what if your life was like GTA 6? Here's how it works. Rule one. Every task in your day is a mission, and every mission pays real money. Rule two. Whatever you buy shows up in seconds, delivered straight to your garage. Cars, boats, helicopters, anything. Rule three. Break the rules, and the police show up. Stars appear in the corner of your eye, one for small trouble, more for big trouble. But you can always lose them. Rule four. A health bar floats over your head. Drop to zero, and you wake up in a hospital, fully healed, a few minutes later. Only Leon sees any of it. Everyone else just sees a kid who suddenly never loses. Day one is done. Let's see how fast he gets rich.

Monday night, Leon has twenty thousand dollars and a problem. The markers don't stop. Tuesday, a yellow circle appears on the neighbor's lawn. Mow it, fifty thousand. Wednesday, a package for a man across town. Deliver it, one hundred and twenty thousand. Thursday, a stranger's yacht needs washing. Three hundred thousand. And Friday, a marker over a neighbor's dog. Walk it for ten minutes, and the number says six hundred thousand. Nobody asks why. By Friday night, Leon's bank app shows one million and ninety thousand dollars. A typical full-time worker in America would need about seventeen years of paychecks to get there. Leon needed five days and one very good dog. He pays off Mom's mortgage. Mom sits down on the kitchen floor and cries into a dish towel. Dad gets the pickup truck he's been staring at for six years. Biscuit gets a golden bowl. Biscuit does not understand the bowl, but he is extremely loyal to it. Owen asks if he can borrow a hundred dollars. Leon sends him ten thousand by mistake. Owen doesn't speak for a full minute. Money is nice. But money isn't why the whole school stops at the fence. The garage is.

Saturday morning, Leon hears a hiss of air, and a matte-black hypercar appears in the driveway, still warm, with a bow on the roof. Leon doesn't read the manual, because the game already told him how to drive it. For scale, in two thousand nineteen, a modified Bugatti Chiron hit three hundred and four miles per hour on a German test track. Leon's is not that one. It's close enough, and it's his. By Sunday, the driveway also holds a speedboat on a trailer and a red helicopter on the lawn. Monday, Leon pulls up to school with the windows down. Owen drops his backpack. Chase drops his sandwich. Zoe walks over, looks at the car, then at Leon. "Is that a Bugatti?" "It's a gift," Leon says. "From me." Ms. Park takes a photo. The principal asks for a ride. And then Leon makes one mistake. He presses the gas a little too hard.

Sirens. One star lights up in the corner of Leon's eye. He ignores it. Two stars. A second patrol car joins in. Three stars, and now there's a police helicopter circling the school, and every kid in the yard is filming. Leon grins. Rule three. You can always lose them. He cuts through the car wash, comes out the other side, and puts on a different cap. The stars blink. Then they vanish. The cops drive past him, slowly, looking for a boy in a blue cap. The whole playground erupts. Leon takes a bow. And that's when his phone rings. Mom. "Leon. Why is there a police helicopter on our street, and where is Dad's other car?" One more star lights up. It's the only one he can't lose. Quick note, this is a cartoon. Don't try any of this at home. But Leon feels unstoppable. And he hasn't even found out about rule four.

Tuesday, the school roof. Owen leans over the edge. "Bet you won't jump." Leon looks down at the pool. It's drained for repairs. He jumps anyway. A bar floats over his head and drops fast, ninety, forty, three percent. Then gray letters across the whole sky. Wasted. And then, a beeping machine, a white ceiling, a nurse with a cup of hot chocolate. His bar is back at one hundred percent. The doctor flips through the chart. "Zero broken bones. Zero. I checked twice." Four minutes. That's all it cost him. The bill goes to Dad, eighteen thousand dollars. Dad stares at it, then looks out the window at his new truck, and says, "Worth it." By evening, the video of the jump has seven million views. Leon is the most wanted kid in town, and in a good way. Everybody wants to be him. Wednesday morning, Biscuit's golden bowl is full. Biscuit is gone.

The back door is open, and the leash is still on its hook. Mom is in the kitchen, calling the neighbors, her voice shaking. Dad is searching the garage. Leon looks at his bank app, more than a million dollars, and understands that none of it can find one small dog. And on Leon's map, a red marker pulses, far outside town, at the old amusement park. Mission. Rescue Biscuit. Reward, none. Leon reads that twice. It's the first mission in his life that pays nothing, and the only one he has ever wanted this much. Under the marker, one more word. Rival. Leon doesn't say a word. He grabs his cap and walks out onto the lawn, where the red helicopter is waiting. "Leon, where are you going?" Mom calls from the porch. "To work," he says, and for once, it isn't about the money.

A vest of body armor appears in his hands out of thin air, and a second bar slides in above his health bar. The helicopter lifts off, and the whole street watches it climb. The amusement park is a gray skeleton in the evening light, with one wheel still lit. At the very top of the Ferris wheel, a boy in a gold jacket sits with Biscuit on his lap. He has his own helicopter. He has his own health bar. And he waves. The chase takes the roller coaster. Leon dodges the rails at full speed, clips a tower, and falls. Wasted. A white ceiling. Four minutes. He's back, in the air, behind the boy. A second hit, and again, wasted, again white ceiling, again four minutes, and Biscuit is a little farther away every time. On the third try, Leon doesn't dodge. He hovers over the wheel, grabs the ladder, and jumps. He lands in the car, grabs Biscuit, and stares at the boy in the gold jacket, who just smiles. "Nice run," the rival says. "See you on the next map." Then his bar drops to zero, he disappears in a burst of gray, and the wheel goes quiet.

Green letters across the sky. Mission passed. Respect, plus one. Biscuit licks Leon's face for about a minute. Owen asks if the helicopter has a snack drawer. It does. The helicopter lands on the front lawn, and Mom stands there with her arms crossed. "Where did you get a helicopter?" "A mission, Mom." Dad checks the new bill and sighs. "Next time, use the stairs." Leon laughs, and for a second, the whole map seems to hold its breath. Then the minimap zooms out, slowly, and Leon sees how small his town really is, just one tiny corner of something huge. And far away, past the hills and the ocean, one new marker is blinking. Mission. Three question marks. So, what would you do with a life like this?
