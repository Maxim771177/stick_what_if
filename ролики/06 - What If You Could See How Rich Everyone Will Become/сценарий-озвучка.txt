Leon is running for the bus, but he stops at the hardware store, because a man is sitting against the wall with a paper cup and a cardboard sign. Leon has exactly two dollars, his bus money. He drops it in the cup. "Thanks, kid," the man says, and looks up. [short pause] And that's when Leon sees it. A glowing number, hanging in the air above the man's head. [excited] Six billion, three hundred and ten million dollars. Leon looks at the torn jacket. The shoes held together with tape. Then back at the number. Then at his own reflection in the shop window. Sixty-one thousand, four hundred dollars. Leon is ten years old, he is about to walk to school, and he has just lost a money contest to a man with a paper cup. [curious] How? Leon has no idea. But by Saturday, he'll know exactly how, and what it costs to find out.

[curious] So, what if you could see how rich everyone will become? [matter-of-fact] Here's how it works. Rule one. The number above a head is the most money that person will ever have, if nothing changes. Rule two. The number changes the second something else does. A new job, a big choice, one bad day. Rule three. It shows money and nothing else. Not how, not when, and not whether anyone is happy. You can't turn it off. [energetic] Leon sprints home to check his family. [energetic] But then you start noticing the problems. Problem one. The number is the end of a very long road, and you only see the finish line. Warren Buffett built about ninety-nine percent of his fortune after his fiftieth birthday, according to the writer Morgan Housel. So Leon watches an old man feeding pigeons and sees nine million, then a girl on a skateboard and sees two thousand. The mailman has eight hundred thousand. The crossing guard has forty dollars and a very good whistle. Is the skateboard girl doomed? Or is she just at the start? The number never says where anyone is on the road. Only where it ends, if nobody gets in the way.

Problem two. Secrets are heavy, and Leon is terrible at carrying them. On Thursday, he tells Owen. "You're going to be rich. Eighteen million." Owen puts down his screwdriver. "So it's already decided? Then why am I building this?" He walks home. The robot stays on the table, and above Owen's head, the number drops. Eighteen million. Six million. Nine hundred thousand. [matter-of-fact] And this part is real. In nineteen ninety-eight, psychologists Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck gave fifth graders puzzles and praised some of them for being smart. Those kids did worse on the next round than kids praised for working hard. [energetic] Tell somebody they've already won, and they stop playing. Leon fixes it the only way he can. "I made it up, Owen. Forget it." Then he spends two days handing him screws, until the number crawls back to fourteen million. Not all the way. Some things you can't un-say, even with a screwdriver.

[serious] Problem three is the one that keeps Leon awake. The man by the hardware store. All week, his number never moves, and everyone walks around him like he's a mailbox. On one night in January twenty twenty-four, about seven hundred and seventy thousand people in America had no home. [energetic] On Friday, Leon sits down next to him. His name is Walt. He used to repair elevators, until his back gave out, then his job, then his apartment. From his jacket, Walt pulls a wet notebook full of drawings. A machine that cleans dirty water with a bucket, sand, and a car part. "Works on paper," Walt says. "Nobody ever read past page two." Leon asks why he never built it. Walt laughs. "Nobody gives a workshop to a man with a cardboard sign." Leon looks up at the number. It was never about Walt. It's about the notebook.

[tense] Saturday morning, it rains. Leon is carrying a pitcher to the park to open the biggest lemonade stand in town, when he spots Walt behind the hardware store, holding the notebook over the dumpster. Above Walt's head, the number starts to fall. Six billion. Four billion. Nine hundred million. Every step toward the bin, a zero disappears. Leon drops the pitcher and runs, sneakers sliding, rain in his eyes, the notebook the only thing on the street he can see. [excited] "Walt, wait! Give me one day!" The future where he sold lemonade all Saturday was a little richer than this one. Leon decides it's a fair price.

[energetic] The only open door on a rainy Saturday is the school gym, where Ms. Park runs the robotics club. Owen is there, because Owen is always there. Ms. Park flips to page three. Then page four.
