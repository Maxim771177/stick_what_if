Tuesday, second period, room fourteen. Ms. Park walks down the rows handing back math tests. She stops at Caleb's desk and smiles. "Perfect again, Caleb. Everyone, this is what talent looks like." Caleb holds up his paper, a giant red A-plus. Then she reaches Leon and drops his test on the desk without a word. A giant red F. Somebody in the back whispers, "Ouch." Leon looks at Caleb. Floating above Caleb's head, glowing like a neon sign, is a number. Seventy. Leon turns to the window and sees his own reflection. One hundred and sixty. Owen leans over. "Why are you staring at the window?" "Owen," says Leon, "I'm a genius." Owen glances at the F. "Sure you are." So, what if you could see everyone's IQ? And what happens when the numbers and the grades refuse to agree?

Here's how it works. Rule one. Every person has a glowing number above their head, and you're the only one who can see it. Rule two. It's a real IQ score on the standard scale, where one hundred is average and most people land between eighty-five and one hundred and fifteen. Rule three. The number is honest, but it only measures one thing, how fast a brain handles brand-new puzzles. It doesn't measure effort. It doesn't measure homework. And Leon, who has never read a manual in his life, skips that part. All he hears is this. Only about one person in thirty thousand scores a hundred and sixty. Finally, an explanation for why the teachers keep sighing at him.

That morning goes like this. Mom is flipping pancakes, one hundred and four. Dad is asleep on the couch under a newspaper, one hundred and nine. The bus driver is a hundred and one, and he takes the first corner so fast that three kids scream. In the park, a man spends ten minutes fighting a paper map. One hundred and thirty-three. The map wins. A street magician pulls a coin from a kid's ear, one hundred and fifty-two, then drops his whole deck of cards. And the pizza guy on Maple Street, eighty-seven, is hanging his sign for the day, and he makes the best pizza Leon has ever eaten. So Leon starts keeping score. Smarter than the bus driver. Smarter than Dad. Smarter than the pizza guy, but definitely not smarter than the pizza. For the first time in his life, he feels like the smartest kid on the bus. Then the bus reaches school, and numbers turn out not to take tests.

Problem one. The number turns Leon into a snob. At lunch, he scans the cafeteria. Nine hundred students, and the highest number in the whole building, besides his own, is one hundred and thirty-one. He is the smartest kid in school, and he starts acting like it. Owen says, "Hey, the test covers chapter seven." Leon checks the number above Owen's head. Ninety-seven. "I'll be fine." A girl asks him for help with fractions, and he checks her number first, then explains it very slowly. Owen notices. Leon doesn't. Two weeks later, he fails again. His number is perfect, and his grades are a disaster. Meanwhile, every single week, Caleb gets an A-plus with seventy glowing over his head. That isn't possible. Unless Caleb is cheating.

Problem two. Leon decides to catch him. Monday, six-forty in the morning, the school is empty, but the library light is already on. Caleb sits alone with a stack of index cards and a thick green notebook. Leon steps out from behind a bookshelf. "Okay. How are you cheating?" Caleb doesn't even look surprised. "I'm not. And I know my number. They tested me in third grade. Seventy." Leon opens his mouth. Nothing comes out. "About two kids in every hundred score that low or lower," says Caleb. "Mom sat quiet for a minute. Then she bought me a notebook." He opens it. Every mistake he made all year is written inside, with the date, the wrong answer, the right one, and one more line that says why. "Everybody thinks I'm gifted. I'm not. I just leave earlier." And here's the real part. In two thousand and five, the psychologists Angela Duckworth and Martin Seligman followed one hundred and forty eighth graders. Self-discipline predicted final grades more than twice as well as IQ did.

Problem three, and it's the big one. A huge brain skips steps, and teachers grade steps. Leon starts copying Caleb. Index cards. Six-forty. A notebook of his own, with one page in it. On the next test, he gets every answer right, and the paper still comes back with a C. Ms. Park has circled two words in red. "Show work." "It's obvious," says Leon. "Obvious is not a step," says Ms. Park. Half credit. Across the aisle, Caleb writes out every line, even the ones he could skip, then checks the whole page twice. Leon watches him, and for a second, he doesn't see the number at all. He sees a kid in the front row who got there early. Then comes the part nobody expects. In nineteen twenty-one, the psychologist Lewis Terman began following more than fifteen hundred children with enormous IQs, hoping to find the next generation of geniuses. Two kids were tested and didn't make the cut. Their names were William Shockley and Luis Alvarez. Both of them later won the Nobel Prize in Physics. Not one of the fifteen hundred did. Scientists still argue about what that proves, but it proves at least this. A number can predict a lot and still miss the thing that matters. Leon stares at his C, then at Caleb's notebook. And that's when Ms. Park makes an announcement.

The science fair. Every team builds a Rube Goldberg machine, twenty steps of junk that must pop one balloon at the end, live, in front of the whole school. It counts for a third of the grade, and Leon is currently failing. Ms. Park reads the teams. "Leon and Caleb. The highest number and the highest grade. What could go wrong?" Leon sketches a beautiful machine in ten minutes. Dominoes, a toy train, a mousetrap, a bucket, a fan. "Perfect," he says. They test it. It fails at step six. Caleb writes it down. They test it again. Step six again. Third time, step nine. "This is a waste of time," says Leon. By Thursday, they have run it forty-one times, and it has failed forty-one times in thirty different ways, and Caleb has written down every one. "You're not even fixing it," says Leon. "I'm finding where it breaks," says Caleb. "You can't fix what you haven't found." Run forty-two. Twenty steps, one pop. Owen cheers so loud that the janitor looks in. Leon grins. His design was never bad. It was just untested. The show is tomorrow, and nobody has tested the gym.

Friday, the school gym, nine hundred students on folding chairs. The machine sits on a long table with a balloon at the far end. Owen holds the stopwatch. Ms. Park says, "Begin." Dominoes fall. The train rolls. The mousetrap snaps, the bucket tips, and eleven steps go perfectly. Then someone opens the gym door. A draft rolls across the table, and the paper sail at step twelve starts to turn the wrong way. Leon sees it in half a second, the way a hundred and sixty sees things. The marble is going to miss the ramp. He has maybe ten seconds. "Caleb! The notebook! Draft!" Caleb flips pages so fast he nearly rips one. "Run nineteen. Door open, step twelve fails. Fix, a coin on the ramp." "A coin?" "A coin!" Owen digs in his pocket and throws a quarter across the table. Leon catches it, slaps it onto the ramp, and the marble rolls over it and into step thirteen. Fourteen. Fifteen. Seventeen. Nineteen. Twenty. The balloon explodes, and the whole gym erupts. Leon turns to Caleb, and Caleb is already writing in his notebook. "What are you writing?" "Run forty-three. It worked."

Ms. Park gives them an A. Not an A-plus, because Leon's sketch had no labels. He says it's obvious what the parts are, and then he hears himself. Afterward, he finds Caleb in the hallway. "I'm sorry I called you a cheater." Caleb shrugs. "You were wrong. But you checked. That's a start." Leon looks up at the seventy, then at the A in his hand. "Seventy and an A-plus. A hundred and sixty and a C." He thinks about it. "A number tells you where you start." "And the notebook tells you where you end up," says Caleb. Then something strange happens. On Monday morning, Leon walks into class, glances up at Caleb, and freezes. Seventy-one. He checks the window. One hundred and fifty-nine. Numbers are not supposed to move.
