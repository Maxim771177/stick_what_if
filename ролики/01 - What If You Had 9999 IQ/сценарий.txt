The teacher is still reading the instructions. Sixty minutes, no calculators, do not turn the page until I say start. You turn the page. By the time she looks up, the test is finished, all fifty questions, and your pencil is back behind your ear. "Leon, I haven't said start." "Yeah. I'm just waiting for the others." Somebody in the back drops an eraser. And that was the easy part.

So, what if you had an IQ of nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine? Quick reality check first. Real tests stop at around one hundred and sixty, because nobody above that has ever been measured well. So your number doesn't just beat the scale, it breaks it. That's the fun part. Here's how it works.

Rule one. IQ isn't a pile of points, it's a rank. The average person gets one hundred, and every fifteen points is one step up. Even a genius at one hundred and sixty is only four steps above average. You're about six hundred and sixty steps up. You're not winning the race, you're in a different sport. Rule two. Your head is the same size. Same brain, same fuel, a fifth of your body's energy, from the same sandwiches. You didn't get a bigger battery, you got a much better program. Rule three. You think instantly, but your body doesn't. Same hands, same legs, same nerves, and a nerve signal tops out around four hundred kilometres an hour, about two hundred and fifty miles an hour. Fast for a body. Slow for you. And one limit. You can't know what you've never seen. You can work out almost anything from almost nothing, but you're a mind, not a search engine. Ask you about a country you've never heard of, and you'll guess its capital from the climate and the shape of the map. You'll probably be right. But it's still a guess.

Plus. Homework takes nine seconds, and six of those are you looking for a pencil. Plus, you finish the teacher's sentences. Not the short ones, the long ones, the ones with three commas. Plus, Saturday at the grocery store. You look at the crowd once, and you see the shortest path through it, the checkout line that will actually move, and the guy who's about to turn his cart without looking. You're out in three minutes. Mom is still looking for a parking spot. Plus, languages. You learn Spanish on the bus, and by the third stop you're correcting the driver's grammar. Plus, checkers with Grandpa. Every Sunday for years, and he's never lost. Until you win in six moves. Then you let him win the next game, because you're not a monster. He knows. He pats your head and says, "You're getting better." Best Sunday ever. So far, nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine looks amazing. But then you start noticing the problems.

Problem one. You're hungry. All the time. Your brain is two percent of your weight and already burns a fifth of your energy, and yours is working harder than any brain ever has. A normal brain barely spends more when it thinks hard, usually under five percent. Yours would. So at ten fifteen, you're thinking about lunch. At ten forty, you're thinking about a sandwich in extremely high resolution. You can calculate its exact calories, and it doesn't help. You even design a better lunch tray, with a built-in cup holder and a snack drawer. Nobody asked. In the cafeteria, you eat your lunch, then your friend's lunch, then you stare at the lunch lady's apple. "Leon, are you okay?" "I'm thinking." "You look like you're going to eat my tray." The fix is boring. You carry food everywhere. Peanuts in your pocket, a banana under your cap, a granola bar behind your ear. You're a walking snack bar, and you're not ashamed.

Problem two. Your body is late. A cup rolls off the table. In your head, it already happened. You know the speed, the angle, the exact spot where it lands, and you've planned seventeen ways to catch it. Your hand arrives when the cup is already in pieces. Four hundred kilometres an hour sounds fast, until your thoughts are faster. So you're the smartest kid alive, and you're the clumsiest one in gym class. You know exactly where the ball will land, and you're still standing there when it hits your shoe. "Leon, move!" "I'm calculating!" "It's a ball!" The fix is planning ahead. You can't react faster, so you decide earlier. You start moving before the cup falls. Sometimes it looks like magic. Sometimes you're just holding a hand under a cup nobody dropped. Last week you invented a better way to tie your shoes. In theory. In practice, you're still wearing them on the wrong feet.

Problem three. Sleep. And this one is bigger than the others, so stay with me. You find a problem. Not a school one, a real one, a famous math problem nobody has solved in a hundred years, and you can feel the answer is close. So you skip bedtime. Why waste eight hours when your brain works this fast? Midnight. Two in the morning. Four. You're so close. At three, you write a proof that looks beautiful. At five, you notice the first line says two plus two is five. Here's what you don't know. Sleep isn't a pause. Your brain is cleaning. In a study from twenty thirteen in the journal Science, on mice, researchers measured that the space between brain cells opens up by about sixty percent during sleep, and that lets the brain wash out waste, including a protein linked to Alzheimer's. Sleep is also when new memories get locked in. In people, when you skip it, the memory centre, the hippocampus, works worse at saving new things, even when you try hard to stay alert. So at six in the morning, your genius brain has the answer on a napkin, and you can't read your own handwriting. At seven, roll call. "Leon?" You open your mouth. Nothing. Not "here". Not your name. Nothing. "Leon?" The kid next to you whispers, "That's you." "Is it?" That's the problem. The fastest brain in the world, on zero sleep, is just a very fast, very confused brain. The napkin, by the way, says "check the thing." Nothing else. The fix is the hardest one, because it isn't a trick. You sleep. A full night, same time every night, like medicine. Mom puts you to bed, and you can't argue, because you know she's right. You've seen the study.

Problem four. The quiet one. You tell your best friend a joke. A good one, with four layers. He laughs at the first layer. You explain the other three. He stops laughing. You explain why that's also funny. He says he has to go home. At lunch you sit alone with a book about black holes, and a kid asks what it's about. "Everything," you say. He nods politely and sits somewhere else. This part is real. In nineteen forty-two, the psychologist Leta Hollingworth wrote about twelve children with IQs above one hundred and eighty. Her case studies found that kids that far ahead were hardly ever picked as leaders by their classmates, and many struggled to make friends. Nobody was mean. They were just on different pages of the same book. And you're hundreds of pages ahead. There's no clean fix. You learn to say smaller things. You ask questions instead of answering them. You let your friend win at video games, and sometimes it works. Not always. And then one winter afternoon, all of it gets tested.

December. First ice on the pond behind your house. Your little sister Nia is six, and she's wearing mittens with penguins on them. One of them is lying out on the ice, twenty metres from the shore. About twenty-two yards. She steps out to get it. The ice makes a sound. A long, low crack, like a door opening in a very big house. And you know exactly what that means. The rule of thumb is to stay off until there's at least four inches of clear ice, about ten centimetres. This isn't that. You can see it in the colour. Your brain runs the numbers in one second, her weight, the ice, the distance, the time before the next crack. And then rule three hits you. Your legs won't get there in time. And the limit hits you too. You've never seen a rescue. You can guess one, but a guess is not good enough. You could throw a coat, a stick, a snowball. Your brain lists forty-one options in a blink, and every single one needs your arms to be faster than they are. So you don't run. You keep your voice calm. "Nia. Stay exactly where you are. Look at me. Don't move." She freezes. Then you shout the smartest thing you've ever shouted. "Help! Somebody! The pond!" A porch light turns on. Then another. Then somebody's running with a coil of rope. Three neighbours, all of them slower than your brain, all of them faster than your legs, and one of them has done this before. You stand there with a clear head, because you ate, because you slept. For the first time in your life, all that brainpower has one job. Know when to call people.

It takes four minutes. A rope, a firm voice, and a neighbour who knows what he's doing. Nia is back on the shore in a blanket, shaking, but fine. "I told you I'd get the mitten," she says. You look at the pond. The mitten is still out there. It stays there until spring. Your brain solved a thousand problems this week. Your sister solved the only one she cared about, and lost the mitten.

That night you go to bed on time. You're asleep in nine seconds. The next morning, there's a note on your desk, in handwriting you don't recognise. "I saw your test."
