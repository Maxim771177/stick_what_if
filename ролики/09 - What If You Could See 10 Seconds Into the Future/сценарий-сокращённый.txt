Lunch line, Tuesday, twelve-oh-five. Leon is holding a tray of tomato soup when he just stops. His eyes go glassy. His mouth hangs open. Owen waves a hand in front of his face. "Dude? Hello?" Three seconds later, Owen's tray slides toward the edge of the table, heading straight for his lap. Leon's hand is already there. The soup stays in the bowl. "How did you do that?" Leon doesn't answer, because something stranger just happened. Ten seconds ago, he heard Owen say that exact sentence. Before Owen said it. So, what if you could see exactly ten seconds into the future? And what happens when you see something you can't let happen, and you only have ten seconds to stop it?

Here's how it works. Rule one. You see exactly ten seconds ahead, like a short movie in your head, and then you snap back. Rule two. Your body stays normal. You know what's coming, but you are not any faster. Rule three. The future isn't written. Change one thing, and the whole movie updates. Rule four, the one Leon finds out in math class. Looking costs time. Every glimpse freezes him for about three seconds, and those seconds come out of the ten. "Leon," says Ms. Park, "are you asleep?" "No, ma'am. Your marker is about to die." The marker dies. So Leon has a choice. Look a lot and live in a daze, or look rarely and stay blind. And being ten years old, he picks a third option. He goes for the money.

Saturday, Hat Trick Sports. A sign by the door says kids penalty challenge, stop four of five shots, win ten thousand dollars. Leon pulls on gloves two sizes too big and walks into the goal. Here's the problem with penalty kicks. From eleven meters, a hard shot reaches the net in about half a second. A goalkeeper needs about that long just to react, so real keepers don't react. They guess, and jump early. Leon doesn't have to guess. Shot one. He looks, freezes, and the referee mutters, "Is he okay?" Then Leon dives left a full second before the kick. Save. Shot two, right. Save. Shot three, left again, and the crowd starts chanting. Shot four. Leon sees the ball heading for the top corner, a rocket from a boy twice his size. He knows exactly where it's going. He dives with everything he has. His fingertips miss it by an inch. Knowing where a ball goes doesn't make your arms any longer. One miss is allowed. Shot five. Look, freeze, dive, save. Four out of five. Ten thousand dollars! That night, Mom holds the envelope and asks one question. "Where did you get this?" "Luck," says Leon. She looks at him for a long time. But money was never the thing Leon wanted most.

Monday after school, Leon walks twenty steps behind Zoe, which is totally normal. She has headphones on and her bag over one shoulder. Then he looks ahead. A scooter. A boy in a gray hood. A hand grabbing the strap, and Zoe hitting the pavement. Leon freezes for three seconds, and seven are left. He sprints. Six seconds. Five. He grabs her sleeve and pulls her backward through an open garden gate. The scooter shoots past, empty-handed. Zoe pulls out one headphone. "What was that?" "You looked like you were about to trip," says Leon, which is the worst lie of his life. She laughs anyway. From that day on, Leon and Zoe walk home together. Every day. He is the happiest kid in the state, and he has no idea what's coming.

Then school gets easy. For the science presentation, Leon checks ten seconds ahead on every slide. A fact that makes Ms. Park nod, he says twice. She gives him the highest score of the year. In gym class, he plays dodgeball, looks, steps left, ducks right, and nine balls miss him in a row. Coach Burke calls him the first kid in school history nobody can hit. By Friday, half the school calls him Lucky Leon. Nobody says smart. Everybody says lucky. And lucky people are not allowed to have a bad day.

Then comes the big question. The Friday dance. Leon wants to ask Zoe, but first, he looks. In the vision, he says, "Do you want to go with me?" and she says, "Sorry, I'm going with my cousin." Leon changes it. Look, freeze, three seconds. "Would you like to go to the dance?" "Sorry, I have a thing." Look, freeze. Zoe waves a hand at him. "Leon? Are you okay?" Third try. He phrases it a completely new way, and in the vision, Zoe smiles and says yes. So he says exactly those words. And Zoe says, "Yes." But on the walk home, she stops. "That was weird. You said the one sentence that works on me. Like you'd already heard my answer." Leon opens his mouth. Nothing comes out. Because she's right. He never heard what Zoe really wanted to say. He only heard what she'd say to a script. And here's the real part. In nineteen eighty-three, the neuroscientist Benjamin Libet and his team published a famous study in the journal Brain. They found that brain activity began about half a second before people said they had decided to move. Scientists still argue about what that means. But if brains decide before we notice, what is Leon reading ten seconds ahead? Their choice, or his own script? And that was the easy problem.

The hard problem is time. Leon's day now has forty glimpses, and every one is three seconds of staring at nothing. The school nurse takes his temperature twice. In the cafeteria, he freezes in the middle of telling Owen a joke, and Owen says, "That's the third time today. Just finish it." Worst of all, he stops hearing people, because he's too busy listening to what they're about to say. The more he looks, the less he lives. A week later, it's the winter concert.

Friday, the school gym, seven o'clock. Above the stage, a steel frame full of spotlights hangs from four cables. Owen stands right under it with his robot, and Zoe stands next to him, holding the plug. Leon sits in the third row with a bag of popcorn. Out of habit, he glances ahead. Three seconds. Then he sees it. One cable snaps. The frame swings. It falls straight onto Owen and Zoe. Leon snaps back. Seven seconds left. Now he has a choice. Run blind and hope, or look again and lose three more seconds. Leon does the math out loud. "Seven. If I run, I get there at three." He runs. Two seconds pass, five left. He looks once, only once, because he has to know who to push. Three seconds gone. Two left. Shove Owen left, and Zoe is under the frame. Shove right, and Leon is. So he doesn't choose. He jumps onto the stage, yells "Everybody move!", throws his arms around both of them, and drags them into the aisle. The frame hits an empty stage with a bang that rattles the windows. Silence. Then somebody laughs, then somebody claps, and the whole gym stands up, because they think it's part of the show.

Afterward, Zoe holds his sleeve, still shaking. "You froze right before it happened. You always do that. What do you see?" Leon thinks about lying. Instead, he tells her the truth. All of it. Zoe is quiet for a long time. Then she says, "So ask me something else. Without looking." Leon swallows. He doesn't look. He says something clumsy that makes no sense, and Zoe laughs and says something just as clumsy back, and neither of them knows what comes next. A plan is only worth the seconds you have left to use it. And these are the best ten seconds of his life, because he didn't see a single one coming.

Spring comes. Leon still looks, but he has learned to count. Then one night in bed, the vision runs a little longer than usual. He counts. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven. The vision is growing, and Leon has no idea what the extra second costs. So, what would you do? Use it for money, for love, or to save a friend?
