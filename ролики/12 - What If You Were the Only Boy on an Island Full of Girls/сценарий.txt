The ferry's engine coughs twice and dies in the fog. Then a reef slides under the hull, and the whole boat tips sideways. Nineteen girls from the school sailing team are hanging off the rail. And you, the only boy, who took the last seat because the boys' team got cancelled. Coach Dunn drops into the lifeboat. "I'm rowing for help! Back in three days! Keep everybody alive!" You grab the one thing within reach, a bag of party glow sticks. Your cap flies off and floats away. Somebody fishes it out of the water with a paddle. It's Ivy, who sits in front of you in science and has never heard you say one single word. She holds your cap and says nothing. So, what if you were the only boy on an island full of girls? Day one. Seventy-two hours to go.

Here's how it works. Rule one. Nobody is coming for seventy-two hours. The radio sank with the ferry, and the nearest phone signal is somewhere in the next century. So you survive on your own. Rule two. Survival has an order, and it's called the rule of threes. About three minutes without air. Three days without water. Three weeks without food. And in harsh weather, about three hours without shelter. It's a rule of thumb, not a law, but it tells you what comes first. Shelter, then water. So while the girls drag branches into a shelter, you stand there holding a bag of party supplies. Rule three. Hazel's rules. She's the team captain, with big curly hair and an even bigger voice. Nobody leaves camp alone. Everybody builds above the tide. She says it twice, the way captains do. Then she looks at your bag. "Glow sticks? Extra weight. Just like the boy." And at noon, she's holding a vote about you.

Noon, the beach. Hazel climbs onto a log like it's a stage. "All in favor of the boy getting his own camp?" Seventeen hands shoot up. Poppy doesn't vote, because her mouth is full of crackers. Ivy's hand goes up last, slowly, while she studies her shoes. The camp's approval of you, zero percent. So you move behind a big rock to boy camp. And honestly, there are perks. One bed, entirely yours. Nobody asks you to clean up. Nobody asks you to share the crackers either, which is fine, because Poppy has them all. And you own a whole palm tree, with a view. Then the palm tree drops a coconut on your head. That night, something wakes you up. Your bed is floating.

Problem one, the tide. You built right next to the water, and the water noticed. Here's the real part. Every beach has a line of dried seaweed and driftwood, called the wrack line, and it shows how high a recent high tide reached. You drag your bed up the rocks, and that's when you spot a second line. Way above the girls' camp, there's a row of whole logs. Those are leftovers from an old storm, and they show how high the sea can really climb. You run over, soaking wet and out of breath. "Hazel, your tents are too low. The ocean has been up here before." Hazel is busy drawing a map of the camp on a flat stone, and she doesn't even look up. "Boy camp isn't on the map." Approval, five percent. You look at the horizon, and the horizon looks back at you, slightly darker than before. Nobody believes the weather until it's wet. By night three, the ocean will be exactly where you pointed.

Problem two, water. Poppy proudly holds up a bottle. "Look! The ocean is full of it!" She lifts it to her mouth, and you smack it out of her hand. Here's why. Human kidneys can only make urine that is less salty than seawater. To flush the salt, you'd lose more water than you drank. Seawater makes you thirstier, not less. "Who made you the expert?" says Hazel. That night you stretch a tarp into a funnel and let the rain do the work. By morning, six bottles of fresh water. Hazel drinks first and says nothing. Poppy drinks second and says, "This tastes like victory." Ivy drinks third and gives you the smallest smile in the history of smiles. Approval, fifteen percent, and you can't stop thinking about that smile.

Problem three, and this one is all yours. On day two, you spot a waterfall on the little island across the channel. You don't ask. You'll prove it. You tie three barrels, two ropes and one very optimistic plank into a raft, and you push off alone, breaking Hazel's first rule in about nine seconds. Halfway across, the raft turns sideways. Then it starts sliding out to sea. A rip current. Here's the real part. Rip currents cause more than eighty percent of the rescues made by beach lifeguards. They don't pull you under. They pull you away from shore. So you paddle against it, harder and harder, and the raft only goes faster. Then you remember something. Don't fight it. Swim sideways. You slide off the raft, swim along the shore, and the current finally lets go. The raft keeps drifting without you, and honestly, it was a terrible raft. Four hours later, you crawl onto the beach, and Ivy is sitting there holding your cap. "You were gone for hours. We thought..." She stops. Then Hazel marches up. "You broke the first rule." Ivy quietly hands you her water bottle, then remembers she's not supposed to talk to the boy, and takes it back. Zero percent. Thirty hours left. You've tried to be a hero three times, and every time you did it alone. That night the wind changes direction.

Night three. Three in the morning, four hours to go. Rain hits the tarp like gravel. You're dragging the glow sticks up to the high rocks, because that's the plan. A giant message in light, a signal no rescue boat can miss. They shine brightest in their first hours, so you can't crack them early. You have to wait until the last minute. You lift the bag, glance back at the beach, and freeze. White foam is already past the seaweed line. It's rolling toward the logs. And right in front of it, nineteen girls are asleep in their tents. If you stop, you lose your signal. If you don't, the ocean takes the camp. You run. You pound on Hazel's tent. Nothing, because she sleeps with earplugs. "Hazel! The water's coming!" "Go back to boy camp!" And then you say the thing you've never said on this island. "I can't do this alone. Please." Silence. Only rain. Then Ivy steps out of her tent with her boots already on. "Move. Now." Poppy is second. Then six girls, then all of them, carrying bags and beds and food up the rocks. Hazel comes out last, with one shoe on, and a wave rolls through her tent right behind her. The spot where her pillow was now belongs to the ocean. Four hours. Three. Two.

Dawn is close, and the fog is so thick that Poppy hugs a rock, thinking it's you. Somewhere out there, a boat engine is growling. There's no flare, and every stick on this island is soaked. But glow sticks are sealed, and your bag is still dry. "Everybody grab a handful! Crack them now, not a second earlier!" Twenty pairs of hands. Hundreds of green lines click into place on the black rock, three giant letters. S. O. S. The engine turns toward the light. "I saw it from a mile away!" shouts Coach Dunn from the deck. Before anyone climbs aboard, Hazel turns to her team. "Who wants to keep the boy?" Nineteen hands go up, and Hazel's is the last. "Fine. You're one of us. Barely." Approval, one hundred percent. Ivy walks over, and for the first time, she looks you straight in the eye. She puts the cap back on your head. "Next time you want to prove something," she says, "ask me first." You nod, because your voice has left the island. It's the longest conversation you've ever had with her, and it was exactly ten words.

On the boat, Poppy asks who has the snacks. Ivy sits next to you, holding the last green glow stick. It's still glowing. Coach Dunn grins. "Good news. The boys' team is back from the flu. Monday, they'll be waiting at the dock, and they've heard all about you." You swallow. Nineteen girls turn around at the same time and look at the dock like it owes them money. "He's ours." So, would you have asked for help sooner? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe, because Monday is going to be loud.
