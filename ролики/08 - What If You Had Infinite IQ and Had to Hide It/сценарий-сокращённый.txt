Monday, lunch, the school cafeteria. On a shelf above the trash cans sits a Rubik's Cube scrambled so badly that the cube club has stared at it for a whole semester. Owen, the club president, sighs at it every day like it owes him money. Two tables away, Leon eats tater tots and glances at the cube once. He solved it before he sat down. But he can't touch it, not yet. "Waiting," Leon whispers to his tater tots, "is the hardest part of being a genius." And by Friday, a man with a clipboard will be hunting for him.

So, what if you had an infinite IQ, and had to hide it? Quick reality check. Real IQ tests stop around one hundred and sixty, and one hundred and thirty already puts you in the top two percent. Infinite isn't a score. It's a problem.

Here's how it works. Rule one. Never sign anything. Rule two. Nobody can ever watch you solve it. Rule three. Solve it ugly. Take the long way, add extra steps, cross out a few things. That last rule sounds easy, until you notice that your brain has no ugly mode. It finds the shortest path every single time, like water finding a drain. That's your one flaw, and it's going to cost you.

Plus, you know what every teacher will say about three words early. You win every argument and lose on purpose. Homework takes exactly as long as tying a shoe. Minus, this semester, three things that nobody could fix got fixed overnight. A broken spreadsheet that ate the whole school fundraiser. A cipher hidden in the library. A math puzzle that stumped the teachers. No signatures. The school paper gave the mystery solver a name, and a reporter named Zoe put it on the front page. Who is the Ghost?

Then this morning, a man in a tweed jacket walked into the principal's office. Mr. Hale, from the state testing bureau. He laid three solutions on the desk and said, "Whoever did this found the shortest possible way every time. Humans never do." He turned to the principal. "Nobody gets that clean by accident. It takes either a machine, or a kid who has spent years hiding." He stared at his clipboard for a long moment. "I only know one person who solves like that." Five days. And he's not leaving until he finds out who.

Back to lunch. The cube isn't just scrambled. It's the superflip, the one position that needs every single move. In twenty ten, researchers proved that any cube can be solved in twenty moves or fewer, and Google gave them about thirty-five years of computer time to do it. The superflip needs all twenty. The cafeteria empties. Leon picks up a lunch tray, hides the cube behind it, and his fingers fly. Twenty turns, in under ten seconds, not one wasted. He sets the solved cube on Owen's seat and leaves a single tater tot on top. Owen comes back and stares. He picks it up like it might explode. "Somebody solved the superflip. We tried for four months!" Then he screams, and half the cafeteria screams with him. Everyone is looking at the cube. Zoe is looking at the empty chair.

Tuesday, the gym. Coach Burke holds up one sheet of paper. "Nobody can fold this more than seven times. Go ahead, try." The whole class tries. Fold, fold, fold, and by the seventh fold the stack is a brick that won't bend. "Told you." That's what everyone believed for a long time. Until two thousand two, when a high school student named Britney Gallivan did the math first, and folded a single piece of paper in half twelve times. Her answer was that you need about one point two kilometres of paper, three quarters of a mile. Leon doesn't say a word. He just slips a note into Owen's gym bag. "One point two kilometres. One direction only." Owen reads it twice and yells, "Toilet paper!" By lunch the class has taped together every roll in the building. The whole school runs out for a week, and nobody dares ask why. Three hours later, twelve folds. Twelve. The gym erupts. Someone hugs the paper. Coach Burke sits down on the floor. The note falls out of Owen's pocket, and Mr. Hale picks it up with two fingers. Pencil, no mistakes, no crossed-out numbers, not one extra step. "Whoever wrote this," he says quietly, "didn't try things. They knew."

Wednesday, the town. For ten years, the road outside your school has been a parking lot every morning. Four hundred cars, one road, and traffic lights that argue with each other. City engineers tried three times and gave up. Leon's bus sits in it for twenty minutes, again. So he takes a napkin and fixes it right there. Every light is timed to turn green a few seconds after the one before it, so a car at the speed limit never stops. Engineers call it a green wave. He folds the napkin and drops it in the mayor's suggestion box at the library. The mayor reads it over breakfast. An engineer checks it by lunch. The city reprograms the lights overnight. Thursday morning, Dad drives Leon to school and doesn't stop once. Not once, in ten years. "It's a miracle," Dad whispers. In the back seat, Leon looks out the window and says nothing. By noon, the mayor gives a speech that thanks "an anonymous citizen with a pencil." Meanwhile, Zoe lays a photo of the napkin next to a photo of the note. "Same pencil," she mutters. "Same crossed seven." Rule one was never sign anything. But a crossed seven isn't a signature. It's a fingerprint.

Thursday, midnight, the old clock tower. At the top is a door that nobody has opened since nineteen eighty-nine, locked with a cipher carved into the frame. Thirty-seven years. A university team gave up on it. Leon knows the night guard's route to the second. Mr. Pike walks the loop in three minutes and forty seconds. Leon slips through the janitor's closet, up the spiral stairs, and across the beams above the clock room. He reaches the door, and a flashlight clicks on. "Took you long enough," whispers Zoe. "I figured the Ghost wouldn't miss this." "How did you find me?" "You cross your sevens." Below them, keys jingle. Mr. Pike is early, and Zoe's flashlight is still on. She clicks it off with a shaking thumb. They dive into the clock's machinery, gears the size of cars, and squeeze between two of them just as the bell hits midnight right over their heads. Their ears ring for a full minute. When the guard leaves, Leon crawls out, studies the cipher for forty seconds, and whispers the whole thing out loud. Zoe writes it down with shaking hands. The door swings open. She turns around. "You're the Ghost." Leon sighs. "I prefer guy who really likes puzzles."

Friday morning, inside the tower room. A small desk, a dusty lamp, one envelope, dated nineteen eighty-nine. Zoe reads it out loud. "To whoever opens this door. I built this lock when I was fourteen, because I was the smartest kid in town, and I was terrified of anyone finding out. I figured the right person would solve it. Don't hide as long as I did. Hale." Behind them, the door creaks. Mr. Hale stands there, holding his clipboard like he forgot what it was for. "Thirty-seven years," he says. "I've waited thirty-seven years for someone to open that door." Leon doesn't move. Zoe doesn't breathe. Hale looks at the letter, then at Leon, then he smiles and tears the top page off his clipboard. "I carved that door myself, one summer, with a hammer and a lot of guilt." "Case closed. I'll report that there is no Ghost, only a very tired inspector." He hands Leon a card with a phone number. "Studies of gifted teenagers show that many of them hide it to fit in. You're not strange. You're just early. When you're ready to stop hiding, call." He walks down the stairs. Zoe gives Leon two thumbs up. "Rule two, broken," she whispers, "but I'm keeping the secret." Rule one, broken. Rule two, broken. Rule three, you never got right.
