The bell rings, and three hundred kids pour into the hallway. Above every head floats a bar, like a health bar in a video game. Blue means plenty. Pink means not much. Leon walks through the crowd like he owns the place, because right now, he kind of does. Ms. Park steps out of her classroom. Above her head, in neat white letters: sixty-one years. "Leon. Where's your homework?" "Ms. Park, you have sixty-one years to forgive me." She stares. He grins. Half the hallway laughs, and nobody knows why. Later that day, Leon walks into his own kitchen, and the grin disappears.

So, what if you could see how long everyone has left to live? Nobody on Earth can do that. Doctors make educated guesses. Worldwide, people live about seventy-three years on average, according to the World Health Organization. But an average is not a bar. An average can't tell you about your teacher, your neighbor, or your little sister. You get the real one, for every single person you meet.

Here's how it works. Rule one. Above every head floats a bar. Full and blue means a long life ahead. Short and pink means time is almost up. Under it, a label in the biggest unit that fits: fifty-two years, three days, five seconds. The bar drains smoothly, a tiny bit every second, slower than anything you've ever watched. Rule two. It's a forecast, not a sentence. Change what's about to happen, and the bar changes with it. Skip your seatbelt, it shrinks. Buckle up, it grows back. Rule three. Everything with a heartbeat gets one. Even the dog. Biscuit has fourteen years, which is more than the cat next door. The cat knows. And one limit. The bar tells you when. It never tells you why. It can say four days. It can't say a flu, a fall, or a bad staircase. You get the clock, not the story.

Plus. Every grown-up in the world is suddenly easier to read. Need someone to carry a heavy bag up three flights? You scan the lobby and pick the guy with the longest bar. Fifty-eight years. He has the time. Plus, the lunch line. Leon always picks the lunch lady with the longest bar, because the others sometimes run out of pizza. Okay, that one isn't science. Plus, Rocco. The biggest kid in school, fists like loaves of bread, and a bar of sixty-three years. Same as everyone else. Suddenly he looks a lot smaller. Plus, the dark. Leon used to hate walking home after sunset. Now every stranger on the street shows a full blue bar, and nothing pink anywhere, so he strolls like it's noon. Plus, Biscuit. Leon finally knows the dog has fourteen years, so he promises her fourteen years of belly rubs. She doesn't understand. She approves. It feels like walking around with a cheat code. You know the score, and nobody else does. Being the only one who knows feels incredible. But then you start noticing the problems.

Problem one. Crowds. Saturday, the county fair. Thousands of people, thousands of bars, bobbing and overlapping and glowing. Scientists have tested how many moving things a person can follow at once. The classic answer, from Pylyshyn and Storm in nineteen eighty-eight, is about four. Later tests got some people up to eight or nine. Leon is looking at thousands. He accidentally stares at a clown for ten full minutes, and the clown asks if he's okay. His head spins, and his ice cream hits the ground. So he makes a rule. Pick one bar, look only at that one, and let the rest blur into fog. It works. Mostly. Crowds stay on the list of places to avoid. And that's the easy problem.

Problem two. You can't tell anyone. Leon tries with Omar, his best friend. "Okay, prove it." "Your bar says sixty-six years." "Everybody's does. That's a guess, not proof." And he's right. Nobody else can see the bar, so nothing Leon says can be checked. Here's the funny part. Even real experts guess wrong. In two thousand, Nicholas Christakis and Elizabeth Lamont asked three hundred and forty-three doctors to predict how long four hundred and sixty-eight very sick patients had left. In the British Medical Journal they reported that, on average, the doctors guessed five point three times too long. Mostly too hopeful. So Leon is better than the doctors. And he can't tell a soul. He can live with that. What he can't live with is problem three.

Problem three. And this one isn't funny. Saturday morning. Leon walks into the kitchen. Mom is pouring coffee. Dad is hunting for his keys. Nora is feeding Biscuit under the table, which she is not allowed to do. And above all four heads, the bars are pink. Not a little pink. Mom, nine months. Dad, eleven months. Nora, four months. Biscuit, nine days. All at once. Biscuit lies on the kitchen floor, barely lifting her head. Nobody looks really sick. Mom has a headache. Dad feels a little dizzy. Nora says her tummy feels funny. "Probably a bug," Mom says. Leon's head is pounding too, but he blames all the staring.

Clue one. By noon, the bars fall again. Mom, three days. Nora, one day. Whatever this is, it's draining everyone at the same speed, and the smallest first. Clue two. Leon checks the obvious. He throws out the cereal, and nothing moves. He switches to bottled water, and nothing moves. He even opens a window, and the bars creep up for a minute, then slide down again. Remember rule two? Remove the real cause, and the bars should jump like a character grabbing a power-up. They don't. Clue three. Dad grabs his keys to buy paint. Through the window, Leon watches his bar climb with every step. Four days. Two months. Ten years. Dad comes back inside, and it sinks again. It's not the people. It's the house. Clue four. What changed? Three weeks ago, a plumber put a new gas water heater in the basement. Leon runs downstairs, two steps at a time. On the wall beside it hangs a carbon monoxide detector. The cover is open. There's no battery inside. It's sitting on the shelf. "It kept beeping at three in the morning," Dad says later. "So I took it out."

Leon knows this one from health class. Carbon monoxide has no color and no smell. In your blood, it sticks to hemoglobin about two hundred to two hundred and fifty times harder than oxygen does. The first signs are a headache, dizziness, an upset stomach. Exactly like a bug. In the U.S., the CDC counted about four hundred and thirty deaths a year from it, not counting fires, in its numbers for nineteen ninety-nine to twenty ten. But Leon has a problem. He can't say, "I see bars." So he says the one thing a kid can say. "We learned about this in school. It's a gas. We have to go outside. Please." Mom looks at his face, at the pale little hands holding her sleeve, and for once, she doesn't argue. She grabs Nora. Dad grabs Biscuit. Leon grabs the door.

Everybody goes out in their coats. Nora thinks it's a game. "Leon, who's winning?" "Biscuit." Mom calls emergency services, and the firefighters arrive in minutes. The meter in the basement beeps all the way up. The firefighter turns to Leon. "Good call. A few more hours, and this would have gone very differently." He looks at Leon for a long second. "Who told you?" "Health class." "Remind me to thank your teacher." And the bars jump back like a health bar picking up a medkit. Mom, fifty years. Dad, forty-eight. Nora, eighty-one. Biscuit, fourteen.

A doctor checks everyone with a quick test. A little oxygen, a lot of juice boxes, and everybody is fine. Biscuit gets a long checkup and a very large bone. Dad puts a fresh battery in every detector in the house. Twice. Mom hugs Leon for way too long. "How did you know?" "Lucky guess." She doesn't believe him, but she doesn't ask again. That night, Leon sits at the dinner table and watches his family eat, argue about the salt, and laugh at nothing. Nobody notices he's staring. Four blue bars. Everyone in this house has more time than they had this morning, and only one person on Earth knows why. That's not a curse. That's a superpower.

Monday morning. A substitute teacher walks into class, a tall man in a green scarf. Above his head, a pink bar. Four hours. He's smiling. He's holding a piece of chalk. And he's looking straight at Leon.
